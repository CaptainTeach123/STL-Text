# STL Text

Put text in **any font** onto an **STL model** – raised or cut in, following curved surfaces – and download a print-ready STL.

Everything runs in your browser. Your models and fonts are never uploaded anywhere.

**Live app:** https://captainteach123.github.io/STL-Text/

## What it does

- **Open any STL** (binary or ASCII). Broken files are **repaired automatically**: near-duplicate vertices are welded, flipped faces fixed, holes closed, inside-out and overlapping shells sorted out. Whatever cannot be repaired is kept as-is (shown in amber) so nothing from your model is lost.
- **Any font:** upload a `.ttf` / `.otf` / `.woff` file (or drop one on the page), pick a font installed on your computer (Chrome / Edge), or use one of six built-in fonts.
- **Lines in different fonts and sizes.** A text is a stack of lines, and every line has its own font and letter height – a big serif title with a small script name under it, laid out and placed as one block. Press Enter for a new line; the width field scales the whole block.
- **Curved lines.** *Curve* bends the text into an arc either way – a positive value arches it (∩, the ends lower), a negative one curves it the other way (∪, the ends higher), up to most of the way round a circle – to follow the sweep of a banner or run round a badge. The value is how far round a circle the widest line reaches. Stacked lines sit on concentric arcs as on a badge, every line round its own arc, so each letter keeps its holes, dots and accents, and left- or right-aligned lines start or end on the same radius. The letters keep their size along the arc and stand square on it, and a plaque or banner plate bends with them. Text too short for the curve asked is curved as far as it can be without its inside folding, a curve that would bring the ends of the text or plate round to meet is held back, and the app says which; letters or gaps squeezed too thin for the nozzle on the inside of the bend are reported like any others.
- **Plaques and banners.** Give a text a backing plate – a rounded plaque or a swallow-tailed banner – that is raised on the model with the text raised on it or engraved into it. Plates follow curves too, so a banner wraps around a cane or a mug.
- **Enhance low-definition models** (often what AI generators produce): *Sharpen edges* makes soft, rounded edges crisp and the surfaces meeting there flat; *Smooth bumps* removes lumps and ripples of up to the relief size from surfaces – each level of a stepped surface on its own, so steps, rivets and lettering stay sharp – and takes out triangle-scale noise; *Boost relief* deepens fine detail – leaves, berries, scrollwork – so it stands out and prints with more definition. Everything is bounded so the model stays a valid, printable solid: the triangles are kept, none is ever flipped, and no point moves further than a third of its local triangle size unless you raise *Max move* (fine-tune) to sharpen wider roundings – except that smoothing may move a point by the height of the bump it sits on, never more than half the relief size. *Edge angle* decides what counts as an edge, *Relief size* how large the detail to boost or the bumps to remove are. Compare with "Show original", undo, or reset at any time; downloads include the enhancement.
- **Find details** looks over the whole model for the details standing on it – berries, leaves, stars, rosettes, sprigs – by how far they rise above a smoothed version of the surface, grouping pieces that belong together, and reads each one by its shape: a tight sphere fit is a berry, a footprint with five points a star, a long one a leaf (spiky: a holly leaf), a long group of pieces a sprig, a round group with a bud a rosette, and anything else a dome with little confidence. Each one is also judged on the model's own mesh for how crumpled its surface is: one that is crumpled over a fair share of it looks messed up, one that is not looks clean. The list shows what each detail seems to be, how big, whether it looks messed up or clean, and how sure the reading is; the messed-up ones with a fair reading start ticked, the clean and unsure ones unticked – untick what should stay, tick what should go, change a wrong guess. **Rebuild** then cuts each ticked detail off the model (a removing spot, down to the surface it stood on) and fuses a clean, generated version of that kind in its place, sized and turned like the original, with a skirt that reaches into a curved surface. Everything added is an ordinary spot or part you can move, resize or delete, in one undo step. *Detail size* tunes what counts as a detail, *Remove all found* just cuts them away, and a clean-up spot can be set to *Remove this detail* by hand.
- **Clean-up spots** for clumped areas (a wreath whose berries and leaves have run together, a soft scroll): add a spot, drag its ring onto the area and set its radius. Inside the spot *Separate details* deepens the valleys between details so they read as separate shapes, *Even out depth* gives weak details more depth and very strong ones a little less, *Boost relief* and the sharpen/smooth amounts work as in the global tool but with a larger movement allowance (about one triangle size by default, raise *Max move* for deeply merged details). The clean-up fades out over the spot's soft edge, several spots can be combined, and downloads include them. A spot works on the model itself, so it is not part of what is added on top.
- **Attach other STL files** as parts: place a second model on the first and choose how it connects – *fused* (sunk into the surface), *fused with a fillet* (a smooth, layered fillet around the foot: the strongest, cleanest joint) or *pegs and holes* (the part is downloaded on its own with pegs, the model gets matching holes with clearance, for printing separately and gluing). Scale it, choose which side touches, turn it freely (tilt, roll and spin, with 90° buttons), sink it into the surface, or use it as a cutter. A part always comes down to rest on the surface under it (on a curved or sloping surface, or after a click that landed a little high), and with *Fit to surface* (on by default) it is pushed in until its body meets the model rather than just its nearest point – a scroll attached by its hollow side wraps the model instead of hovering beside it, while a part resting on narrow supports is never pushed down through them; a tilted or rolled part is left resting on its lowest edge – so it really overlaps the model by its sink; *Consume what it covers* (also for text plates) cuts away the model's details under the part so nothing pokes through it – details up to about 5 mm proud of the part, while a structure passing through it (a cane rising through a plate) is left alone; the view shows the model with the cut applied as soon as the box is ticked, and like a clean-up spot such a part lands when dropped; if anything raised still does not overlap the model, the final result and the download say so instead of silently leaving a loose piece. A part that is not touching the model is shown as a grey wireframe and listed as "not touching"; *Snap to model* lays it on the nearest point of the surface by the side that faces the model (so a banner standing on its edge beside the model ends up flat against it), clearing any tilt or roll.
- **Several texts and parts** on one model, each still editable: pick one in the *Texts & parts* list, change its lines, placement or connection at any time. Undo / redo for everything.
- **Click the model to place** a text; it aligns to the surface you click – to the way the surface faces over a few millimetres around the click, so a lumpy, sculpted surface does not tip the text with the one triangle under the cursor, while the face next to a crisp edge or chamfer is kept exactly. Drag the text to move it, nudge with the arrow keys, snap to a side, rotate.
- **Follows curves.** Text is laid on the surface like a sticker: it wraps round mugs, canes, domes, wavy banners and the inside of rings as far as it is long, with every letter its true size along the surface, standing square on it at a constant height or depth – no flat slab, no letters stretched or floating off where the surface turns away. On a lumpy, sculpted surface the line stays straight as seen from where you clicked, and bumps smaller than about a quarter of the letters' height are laid over rather than followed, so the text neither wanders nor shrinks. Run at a slant across a cane or a mug, it is laid like tape – along the spiral the line really follows, every letter upright on it, rather than fanning out – and over a rounded edge or fillet it carries on across instead of stopping. It stays on the surface you clicked where something else lies in line with it (the back of a thin ribbon bending towards you, a tail folded behind it, a scroll curling over it), crosses ledges and slots, and carries on level where the surface ends. The app says when the text hangs over an edge (and leaves out of the download any letter that would print loose), runs into a floor or wall, runs under an overhang with no room for its letters, would wrap further than about 120° round the back of a curve, or has to stretch on a surface curved two ways (a small sphere).
- **Raised or cut in**, with height / depth, letter height or overall width in mm, letter and line spacing, alignment, boldness, mirror (for stamps) and printer-friendly corner rounding.
- **Printing checks** based on your nozzle and layer height: strokes too thin to print, gaps that will fill in, engraving that would cut through a thin wall, text hanging over an edge or crossing a step, text that doesn't touch the model, raised text shallower than two layers – each with a one-click fix where possible.
- **Split for printing.** A model bigger than your printer is cut into pieces that each fit – the Bambu Lab X1 and P1's 250 mm height and the corner of the bed they keep clear included – with pin holes on both faces of every cut so the pieces line up exactly and glue together easily. The app says when a model won't fit the printer you chose and offers to split it into the fewest pieces that fit – lying any side down (a sign or other thin plate always flat), turned across the bed's diagonal if that is what it takes, each standing on a cut face or something flat of the model's – with the cuts kept away from letters, ledges and thin necks. With *Largest piece* set, the pieces are also no longer than that, even where it takes more of them. The offer reads **Split into N pieces** once that split is worked out (in the background, a moment after the model is opened), *Split to fit* until then. Cuts are straight across X, Y or Z, and you can add, drag, type or nudge them yourself. Each piece gets printed pins (or holes for your own dowels) – or, with *Built-in pegs*, square pegs printed as part of one piece that push into sockets in the other, so there is nothing extra to print or buy – matching engraved labels (*1A* ↔ *2A*) and is saved turned the way it prints best; signs and other thin plates get dovetail tabs instead. A cut can also be one that **comes apart**, never glued – a lantern's top that clicks on and off over the light inside, a box's lid – placed by clicking the model where it should come apart, or at one of the spots the app suggests. Each piece has a *print check* line – supports, step lines, what it stands on – and *As printed* shows every piece lying as its file lies on the bed, with the faces that may need supports in red and the step lines drawn on. The download is one zip: the pieces, a sheet of pins with a spare (none with built-in pegs), and a `how-to-glue.txt` with the print list and the glue order.
- **Model tools:** convert inches to millimetres, stand a Y-up model upright, rotate, centre on the build plate, simplify huge meshes, and scale it: the *Scale* box shows and sets the model's size as a share of it as loaded – 100 % is as downloaded, so 400 is four times that whatever size it is now (*Inches → mm* only puts the file's numbers in mm, so the box still reads 100 after it) – with its height at the size typed in its tooltip; *Apply* frames the view on the model at its new size, as *Make it N %* does, and what the status line said of the old size (a fit test's ring and turn) goes. The app suggests these when a file looks like it needs them.
- **Clean solids.** Text and parts are merged with real boolean operations ([Manifold](https://github.com/elalish/manifold)), so the result is one watertight body – not overlapping shells. The heavy work runs in a background thread; the view stays responsive even with large models, and models above ~400k triangles are shown through a lighter preview while downloads keep the full detail.
- **Built for big models.** Every step of the model's derivation (transforms, simplification, enhancement, each clean-up state) is cached, the view is refit rather than rebuilt when only vertices move, and the solid for downloads is built only when needed, so nudging a slider costs one short re-derivation instead of several seconds. Enhancement and clean-up amounts apply when a slider is released (the model dims and the card says *applying…* while the worker catches up). On a dense model the lighter preview is cleaned up alongside the full model, so a spot's effect shows immediately; the download is always computed on the full-detail model.

## Using it

1. **Model** – *Open STL…* (or drag a file onto the page). The info line shows its size and whether it needed repair; the app assumes millimetres, like most slicers.
2. **Texts & parts** – *Add text*, then type; or *Add part (STL)…* to attach another model. Each entry in the list keeps its own settings and placement.
3. **Text & fonts** – one row per line: its wording, font and letter height in mm. *Add line* (or Enter) adds a line below the current one; uploaded fonts apply to the current line. *Text width* scales all lines together.
4. **Raised or cut** – choose *Raised* or *Cut in* and the height / depth; optionally a *Backing plate* (plaque or banner) with its thickness and margin. Warnings about printability appear here. For a part this card offers *Add* or *Cut out*, and the *Part* card its scale, attach side, tilt, sink depth and connection (fused, fused + fillet, pegs & holes).
5. **Placement** – click the model where the text should go, or drag it. The green ring shows where a click would land.
6. **Split for printing** – only for a model bigger than your printer, one you want in parts, or one whose top should come off: *Fit to my printer* cuts it into the fewest pieces that fit, *+ Cut* adds a cut of your own. See [Splitting into pieces](#splitting-into-pieces).
7. **Show final result** (top right of the 3D view; *Show pieces* once the model is split) computes the real merged model; **Download STL** saves it – **Download N pieces (.zip)** for a split model.

The final model is always *model + everything raised or fused − everything cut*, whatever order you added them in, so cut-in text always cuts through raised text. Parts connected with pegs are not merged: they are downloaded as separate files.

The page has a dark cyberpunk look, in which raised text is drawn magenta and cut-in text acid green. Tick *Classic* at the top of the panel for the plain look, light or dark as your system is set (raised text orange, cut-in text pink-red); your browser remembers the choice. Every other colour in the 3D view means the same in both.

### Connecting parts well

- **Fused** is right for one-piece prints: the part sinks a little into the surface (default 0.4 mm) so the two bodies share material.
- **Fused + fillet** adds a concave fillet around the foot of the part, built from layers so it prints exactly as drawn. It spreads the load over a wider footprint, hides small gaps on curved surfaces and looks like a cast joint. 1–3 mm radius suits most parts.
- **Pegs & holes** is for parts printed separately (another colour, another orientation): pegs on the part, holes in the model with a clearance (0.15 mm is a good start for a snug fit after printing), glued on assembly. Pegs are only placed where a whole peg fits under the part (one under each foot of a bridge-shaped part), and the part file is saved lying on its face with the pegs pointing up, ready to print. Pegs need a watertight model to make holes in; on a model with gaps the part is fused instead.
- **Tilt** pivots the part about the point you clicked: one edge goes into the surface, the other lifts off it. The app warns when the lifted edge leaves a gap, which a bigger sink or the fillet closes.
- **Cut out** (a part used as a cutter) removes the part's shape from the model; its *Cut depth* is how far it sinks in. A cutter has no connection options.

### Splitting into pieces

- **Your printer.** Choose it under *Printing* – a generic 220 × 220 × 250 mm printer (Ender-3 class) to start with, a few Bambu Lab and Prusa printers, or type the bed's size – with the margin to keep clear round the edge of the bed for a brim or the purge line. The Bambu Lab sizes are Bambu Studio's own: *X1 / P1S / P1P* prints on 256 × 256 mm up to 250 mm high and keeps the bed's front-left 18 × 28 mm clear, as Bambu Studio does (a small hint under the printer says so); *A1 / P2S* takes 256 × 256 × 256 mm with nothing kept clear. A printer saved as the old *A1 / P1S / X1* opens as *X1 / P1S / P1P*, the smaller of the two: choose *A1 / P2S* if that is yours. A model bigger than the printer says so in its notes and in a toast, and a plain download of it points you to step 6 if it can be split.
- **Largest piece**, under *Margin*, is how long a piece may be along each side when *Fit to my printer* plans it, whatever the printer would take: smaller pieces take less room on the bed and print with less to go wrong, even if there are more of them. Leave it blank for pieces as big as the printer takes; it is 30 mm at least. It is saved with the printer, choosing another printer leaves it as it is, and the split card shows it: *Your printer: 256 × 256 × 250 mm, front-left 18 × 28 mm kept clear · Largest piece 150 mm*. A model the printer takes whole but that is longer than that says so in its notes (*longer than your 150 mm Largest piece*) and is offered a split.
- **The X1 / P1's corner.** A piece fits that printer when it fits beside the corner it keeps clear (228 × 246 mm at the 5 mm margin) or behind it (246 × 218 mm), turned on the bed if need be; a footprint that would fit only by reaching into the room round the corner counts as too big. A piece that uses the room beside or behind the corner still reaches into it when the slicer drops it in the middle of the bed, where Bambu Studio refuses it (*too close to exclusion area*): the notes, its row in the pieces list (*move clear of the corner*) and the glue sheet's print list name such pieces – move them clear of the corner in the slicer. So does a model the printer takes whole only beside or behind the corner: its notes, *Fit to my printer* and the download's status line say to move it clear of the corner. The sheet of pins is laid out so that it clears the corner even when the slicer drops it in the middle of the bed.
- **Fit to my printer** (or *Split into N pieces* in the model's notes) works out the fewest pieces that fit and where to cut: where the cut crosses little and the face has room for pins, away from letters, ledges and thin necks. Every piece must stand on a cut face or on something flat of the model's, so a cone is cut into more pieces than its box would take, as those would stand on a point; a box with nothing in it (the corner of an L) is no piece; and of two ways to cut it, the one whose pieces need less support wins – a U bracket is cut so both pieces stand on their feet rather than lie with a leg held over the bed. The cuts also go where the boxes between them hold few pieces and no slivers: through a table's legs rather than just inside its apron, which would leave a sliver of the apron as a piece of its own. And they go where what they leave holds on: a cut that would leave a bit of the model loose, or holding on only by the few millimetres next to the cut – a ribbon fused to a column over a short stretch – costs about as much as one through letters, so on a column with a ribbon wrapped round it Fit cuts 10 mm off the middle, where both ends of the ribbon hold on over 15 mm or more, rather than through the middle, where one would hold on over 6 mm. A model standing on a base plate – a terrain tile, a diorama, a figure's base, a box's floor – is cut, as a plaque is, so that its pieces can lie flat on the plate, even where that takes more of them: stood on a cut edge, a piece of the plate would be a thin wall. Fit's cuts replace any you had, in one undo step (pressed again with nothing changed, it changes nothing); on a model that already fits (after you chose a bigger printer, say) it takes them away the same way and says so. With *Largest piece* set, Fit keeps every piece within it along each side – a built-in peg's or a lip's length counted on whichever piece gets it – even where that takes more pieces, and the notes say so (*Every piece is within your 150 mm Largest piece.*). Where a detail you keep whole is longer than that, the pieces in line with it may be as long as the detail needs along it – a little longer than the detail, never more than the printer takes – and the notes say which pieces and why (*… except pieces 2 and 5, up to 190 mm along X to keep Ribbon (172 mm) whole*). More cuts do not mean cuts in worse places: they keep away from letters, ledges, thin necks and parts that would hold on only next to a cut as before, and where the fewest pieces within your Largest piece would need such a cut, a plan with a few more pieces (at most half as many again) that needs none wins. A lid that comes apart is the size it is – one thinner than a piece with pins may be is not what stops Fit keeping the other pieces within your *Largest piece*. Where pieces that small would take more than 64, Fit plans the smallest pieces 64 allow and says what to set *Largest piece* to; where no plan keeps within it (and the details you keep whole), it plans for the printer alone and says so – unless those pieces turn out within it all the same.
- **Keeping a detail attached.** A thin detail fused to the body over a short stretch – a ribbon round a column, a handle, a tail – is easy to cut off by accident, and three things look after it. A bit a cut would leave loose that hangs on the piece across the cut stays on that piece, in one part with it (see *The download*). A part left holding on only by the few millimetres next to a cut is said in amber in the cut's row and in the notes, with where to move the cut so that it holds on further from it; *Fit to my printer* keeps clear of such places by itself where it can. And **Keep whole…**, beside *Fit to my printer*, marks a detail no cut may run through: click it on the model and the app finds it – the thin material a 6 mm ball rolled inside the model cannot reach, less the 3 mm skin of the body it stands on (both in proportion on a model longer or shorter than 260 mm, so a model scaled up or down finds the same detail) – tints it green, draws the box round it and lists it under the buttons, *Detail 1 · 148 × 58 × 42 mm*, with a name you can type over and × to let it go (one undo step each). A click on the body says so and lets you click again, a click on a detail already kept whole says so and shows its row, and where only part of a detail was found the status line says to click its other end too; Esc stops. *Fit to my printer* then never cuts through the box, even where that takes more pieces, and the notes say *Ribbon stays whole, on piece 2*; where no plan for your printer keeps it whole (or one of several: the others are still kept), it cuts as it would have and says which cuts run through it and how big a piece it would need. A cut of your own through it says *through Ribbon – asked to keep whole* in its row, in amber, and the download's notes say so too; no note tells you to move a cut into it – where that is the only way to hold a part on further from a cut, the note says so and what to do instead.
- **The offer's number** is the number of pieces Fit's download holds – the model is cut where Fit says, so a box the cuts leave empty is no piece and the loose shelves of a bookcase that print apart are counted: the split is worked out in the background when a model is opened or changed, or the printer or joints change, once the engine has nothing else to do, and a click on the offer then takes that split at once. Moving or retyping text doesn't work it out again – the preview of the next keystroke would wait for it – so the offer reads *Split to fit* until clicked; on a dense model (one shown through a lighter preview) it always does.
- **Cuts of your own.** *+ Cut* adds one across the middle of the longest piece. Every cut has a letter – on its plane in the view, in its row in the card and on the glue sheet. Its row says how much it cuts and its joint: pins, pegs, dovetails or glue only – a cut planned for pins or pegs says glue only once the pieces, the download or the fit test find that none fits its faces – or, for a cut that comes apart, how it holds (*clicks on (4 clips)*, *twist-lock (turn 10°)*, *lifts off*, *no lip*). Once the pieces are worked out it also says, in amber, what of the model it leaves loose (*leaves 15 cm³ loose*) and what it leaves holding on only by the few millimetres of its piece next to it, where it may snap off (*16 cm³ held over only 6 mm*): the notes say where it is, and where to move the cut so that it holds on further from it – and *Fit to my printer*'s own cuts are checked the same way. Change its axis or type its position in its row, drag the handle beside its plane in the view (a readout gives the length of the pieces either side, dovetail tabs and built-in pegs counted, red when one won't fit your printer), or select it and nudge it with the arrow keys (Shift for five steps); Delete removes it, Escape lets go of it. Meanwhile the model is coloured by piece, and a piece too big for the printer turns red; the download button counts the pieces the cuts make, and a box whose loose parts print apart says how many pieces it makes (on a dense model, which takes seconds to cut, the button counts the boxes until the pieces are shown or downloaded). With *Largest piece* set, a piece longer than that says *over 150 mm* in the pieces list – *over 150 mm to keep Ribbon whole* where a detail kept whole is why – but a cut of your own is never refused for it: only the printer makes a piece too big. Up to 12 cuts and 64 pieces.
- **Cuts that come apart.** Tick *Comes apart* in a cut's row for a joint you never glue – a lantern's top you take off to change its light, a box's lid. Its plane turns teal. The piece that can print standing on its far end with the cut face up gets a lip – a band 6 mm tall and 1.6 mm thick round the inside edge of its wall (on a solid face, in a groove round its middle) – and the other piece a groove for it, with the clearance (0.15 mm) of room each side: the outside of the model is unchanged and the opening isn't narrowed. The lip has two to eight spring clips, each a bridge over a slot with a small catch that clicks into the other piece, and how they hold is the cut's choice. *Locks*, the default for a cut you tick, holds firmly: its clips are shorter, stiffer 30 mm bridges, and the side of each catch that holds is steep while the side the top goes on over is shallow, so the top presses on with a click and comes off only with a firm pull – about 5 kg on a lantern's four clips, worked out (seated, the top hides its lip, so there is nowhere to lever it off). *Clicks on* has 40 mm bridges with a plain catch: it holds the top on when the model is carried by it, and pulls off by hand (the notes say when what hangs below may be too heavy for a cut's clips, and their button sets the gentlest hold whose clips would hold it up). *Light click* has smaller catches, for a top taken off often; *Lifts off* has none. A small gap in the lip lets the top go on one way round only. The clips are 1.6 mm thick whatever the nozzle – a wider nozzle's thicker lip is thinned from the inside at each clip – so they hold as firmly with a 0.6 or 0.8 mm nozzle; past that they are two lines thick and a lock's bridges longer to match, so a lock holds as firmly up to a 1 mm nozzle. The lip prints standing up and the groove's roof steps up at 45°: no supports (each clip is a bridge over a slot: leave bridging on, as it is by default; the steep side of a lock's catch overhangs by at most 0.5 mm, which the printer draws out over the air). A wall needs 2.7 mm for a lip; a lip with clips needs about 9.4 mm of plain wall past the cut in the other piece, before a roof, a ledge or an opening (or the next cut), and a straight stretch of 46 mm for each clip. Where there isn't that room the lip is lower or has no clips, and where moving the cut would give it the room – off the top edge of a lantern's windows, down out of a jar's shoulder – the notes say where and their button moves it there; the place is tried first, so the lip there is as good as the wall allows. A lip goes on a piece that would have to print on a curve with it (a lantern's roof on its handle) only when no such move lets the other piece carry it. In a box of compartments the lip runs round the outside wall, past dividers too thin for a groove each side, whose ends it just notches. Where two cuts that come apart cross, or lie a few millimetres apart, each lip keeps clear of the other's; a face whose pieces already lie another way for the other cut's lip just rests, and the notes say so. Where the wall takes no clip at all – a round lantern's, a small round part's, any outline with no straight stretch for one – a cut that locks or clicks on gets a collar twist-lock in their place where one fits, even where no lip has room (through a round lantern's eave): the piece the lip would stand out of gets a collar under its face and a neck with three lugs, the other a socket with L-slots and click tabs, cut into it as it lies on its cut face (only a piece that prints as well so takes it). Set the top on turned a little to the left, so the wide lug finds its wide slot, push down and turn it right until it clicks; turn it left until it clicks again to lift it off. It is centred on the face, which must be one piece, and fits where the wall under the cut goes round it deep enough to carry the collar and the top goes round it for 6.8 mm over the cut; nothing it cuts comes within two nozzle widths of the outside. *Lifts off* keeps the lip. A cut through a thin plate gets unglued dovetail tabs instead, and one with room for neither just rests on the other piece. The pieces list names the piece with the lip and its clips and the one that comes off it; the cut gets no pins or labels, whatever the joint settings, which are then only for the glued cuts. The glue sheet glues every other cut first and says last: *Do not glue cut A: it comes apart. Press the top on until it clicks – it locks; pull hard to open it.* *Fit to my printer* plans its cuts around the ones that come apart and never moves or removes them, and with them alone the model may already fit.
- **Placing a removable part.** *+ Removable part*, next to *+ Cut*, is for a part that comes off – a lantern's top, a box's lid: click the model where it should come apart, and a cut across Z goes exactly there, ticked *Comes apart* (locking), selected and measured, as one undo step. Shift+click places another; Escape, the button again, or taking up anything else (a text, the pieces view, another model) stops it, and a click meanwhile never places text. The crosshair in a cut's row places that cut the same way, along its own axis. While you pick, a plane follows the pointer with the model's section drawn on it – teal where a full lip with clips fits, amber where only a shorter or clipless lip does, red where none does (light for a glued cut, or until the lip is worked out, a moment after the pointer rests – even while the spots are still being looked for) – and a readout beside the pointer says where the cut goes and what it gets there: *Z 190.5 mm · locks with 4 clips · top 101 mm comes off*, or *Z 196 mm · no room for a lip – move down to 190.5*, and names any letters it would run through (*through “LIGHT”*). Where the twist-lock takes the clips' place the readout says so (*twist-lock, 3 lugs, 6 clicks – turn 10°*), and a move is said to be *for clips* only where the lip there has them – on a round wall, where no clip fits, it is *for a twist-lock*, or *for a 6 mm lip* where none fits there either. It snaps to the model's seams (a ledge, where a roof meets the body, a groove round a lid, where straight sides start to slope, the top or bottom of the openings, where the walls start), to the spots suggested and to the nearest place a full lip fits, from 3 mm or 12 pixels away, whichever is more: ticks beside the plane show where, and the readout says what it snapped to (*snapped to the ledge at 200 mm*). The plane, the readout and what a click places stay the same place: zoom or turn the view under a still pointer, or let a target come up beside it (where a full lip fits, once the lip is worked out), and the plane goes there at once. Hold Alt to place it freely. A click where a cut already is places nothing, and the readout says so (*cut A is already here*). Change the cut's hold, the clearance or the nozzle meanwhile and the readout says what it gets now. Dragging the handle of a cut that comes apart snaps the same way – never onto another cut: dragged there, the readout says so, and dropped there the cut stays where it was – and its readout says the lip it gets there; Escape puts a cut being dragged back. A glued cut snaps to none of these: its handle goes by the step, and its crosshair places it where you click. On a touch screen, where nothing hovers, touch the model and slide your finger to move the plane – sliding off the model's edge leaves it where it last was on it – lift it to read the readout, along the top of the view under its own buttons, and tap *Place cut here* (or touch the model again elsewhere; *Cancel* stops); two fingers still zoom and pan the view, leaving the plane as it was. On a dense model the section is drawn from the lighter copy the view shows; the lip is worked out on the model itself.
- **Parts that come off on their own.** A lantern's top is often inside its handle: a cut across the whole model there would take the handle off with the top. *+ Removable part* and *Suggest spots* also list, in a card *Parts that come off*, the parts that come off on their own – a lid inside a handle (the dome and its cap, the handle staying on the lantern), a top with nothing over it, a base from underneath – each as a circle on a face across Z: the material inside the circle from its face up to where the circle is first empty comes out, and everything round it, the handle's legs and crown included, stays on the body. The face is the lowest one on the printer's layers where the lid's edge still prints apart from the handle's feet (two nozzle spreads and the clearance each side) and the face is one piece, and the circle is the middle of the band that is free there; a part that can't come out – lifted and slid every way – is never listed, and a model that is open at the top already or solid all through says so. Each says what it gets at this size: *Dome and cap – twist-lock · Ø 42 mm opening, takes a 38 mm tea light · the handle stays*, whether the collar the lock adds under it asks for support (*the lantern's collar needs the dome's support*, as the download's notes say), and *Use*. A base is listed whether or not its lock fits at this size: too small for one, it says so as an offer – *Base – twists off underneath once the model is 215 % or bigger* – and why not now (*its bottom is Ø 19 mm across, and the lock needs Ø 39 mm*), from what size a tea light goes through it and what the size it offers gives it (*at 300 % it twists off and takes a tea light*), with *Make it 300 %* as its button; it has no *Use* until then, as it would just drop out. Every part's row offers the sizes still to come for any of the lantern's parts – where a twist-lock, or a 38 mm tea light through one, first fits it, each up to a whole 100 % – so the base's row offers *Make it 300 %* (its lock) and *Make it 400 %* (where the dome takes a tea light too), and at 300 % still *Make it 400 %* beside *Use*: a bigger print is a click away on the row of the part being taken out; each says in its tooltip how tall the model is then and what that size gives. Where no lock fits it at any size the finder looks at, it says what stops one – its bottom too thin, no room for the click tabs, its wall too thin, or raised text or a foot standing under its bottom where the lock goes (*engrave the text, or move it off the bottom*) – read alike before and after the final result is built; and a model turned on its side since it was loaded, which lists no part, is told how to stand it up again. A generated model's soft or ragged bottom edge, which grows with the model, never counts against its base's lock, so made very big (*Inches → mm* on a model in mm) it still twists off. The card opens at the foot of the panel: pressing *+ Removable part*, *Suggest spots* or a size scrolls it into view above the bar of Undo and Download – its last part, the base, in full where all of it does not fit – and *Use* scrolls to the part's new row, with the card where both fit. Where no lock fits a lid at this size – a small print's roof too thin for one – it says why (*its roof is 0.7 mm thick*), from what size one fits and from what size a 38 mm tea light goes through, and offers those sizes: *Make it 300 %* scales the model (to that share of its size as loaded, its height in the tooltip) and places its parts again for the new size, as one undo step; *Use – it just lifts off* takes it out anyway, and it locks once the model is made big enough, with no other click; the sizes stay offered once it is taken out. *Make it N %* keeps what you were doing: picking a part on the model goes on, and the pieces view shows the pieces again at the new size. While you pick on the model, the part under the pointer lights up: gold from its face to where it ends, its circle on its face and its walls faint above, and the handle it leaves on the lantern outlined in blue; the readout says what it gets (*Dome and cap come off · twist-lock, 3 lugs, turn 19° · Ø 42 mm opening – takes a 38 mm tea light · the handle stays*, or over the handle *The handle stays on the lantern – click to take off the dome and cap under it*), and over a place in the lantern's top half where a cut across would take the handle with the top, it says that first, and the plane is never shown there as a good place. A click takes the part out as the finder places it, as one undo step (Shift+click picks more; a part already taken out says which cut takes it, and a click on it selects that cut); hold Alt to place a cut across instead. Low on a lantern with a handle – its lowest fifth, where a cut across would take the rest of it off as its top, handle and all – the walls round its base pick the base too, whether its lock fits at this size or only at a bigger one (one too small for its lock gives way there to a spot the app suggests for a cut across – a full lip with clips below the openings, say – where the plane snaps onto it), and so does what stands on its floor inside its circle on any model. A base too small for its lock lights up and its readout says from what size it twists off (*make it 300 % to take it out*): a click there cuts nothing, but says so and lights its row in the card, its size to press (on a touch screen, *Make it 300 %* in the readout); once a size is made – or undone or redone – or placing stops, the page says what is true then instead, at once. A click while the parts are still being found waits for them, and then takes what it landed on. It is a lookup on the card's table, so it is instant even on a model of millions of triangles. A part's row has its letter and name, *Z* (where its face is; none for a base, whose face is the model's bottom), *Ø* and how it holds – *Twist-lock* or *Lifts off*; where no lock fits at this size *Twist-lock* is struck through and says from what size one does, and the part shows as lifting off until then. The circle follows what you type in *Z* or *Ø*; the part changes once you press Enter or leave the box. A face typed in or moved with the arrow keys goes onto the printer's layers, then up to where the part prints apart, and the row and the status line say why it moved (*the circle runs too close to the handle's feet at Z 85.2 – moved to Z 86*); a width typed in, or dragged with the white knob on the selected part's circle, is kept in the free band round it – onto the finder's own width from a few pixels away – and at the band's edge the page says *Ø 52.2 is as wide as fits here*; let go where it reads as it did, nothing changes. A part the finder placed is placed again when the model is scaled, the clearance changed or the printer's nozzle or layers. The row's crosshair picks the part again on the model, moving its cut to the part clicked. On the model each part's circle is teal where its lock fits, amber where it only lifts off and red where it can't come out, with three marks where the lock's lugs are.
- **The twist-lock.** A part that locks twists on with a bayonet and a click, made in the pieces themselves, with nothing to buy or glue. A lid's comes in a collar build: the body gets a collar under the cut, sloping out as the model beside it does, a neck and three lugs (one wider, so it goes on one way round only), and the lid a socket ring with L-slots and click tabs that snap over a ridge as it turns. A base's comes in a plug build: the lantern gets L-slots and grooves in its bore, the base the lugs, the click tabs and a coin slot underneath; every face it adds is 45° or kinder. To fit a lid set it on turned a little to the left, so the wide lug finds its wide slot, push down and turn it right until it clicks; to open it turn it left until it clicks again, lift it a few millimetres and slide it out – under the handle, square to it, on a lantern. A base goes in from underneath the same way, with a coin in its slot. The notes give the turn, the lift and the way out for your model, say how each piece prints – the lid on its cut face, the base on its bottom; the slots and click tabs are bridges (the note gives their length, about 10.5 mm at a 0.4 mm nozzle; turn on *don't support bridges* if your slicer puts support in them), and the collar under a lid needs whatever support the dome itself needs – and offer *Download fit test*: for a lid, two rings with the same lock at the model's size; for a base, a ring of its bore with its slots and a short plug with its lugs, click tabs and coin slot. The click tabs are longer with a wider nozzle, so they bend no further. The pieces list says which piece has the neck and which the socket. In the pieces view the lock's own faces are orange, its lugs yellow (the collar and socket ring, which carry it, a lighter orange), and they glow while you point at the part's row or either piece, the pieces fading round them so all of the lock shows; the *Spread* slider moves the part out along its real way out – turned free, lifted, slid – with what it is doing written under the view, and *▶ Show how it comes apart* plays it, seen from the side where the part slides out along the line of sight (Shift: how it goes on, with the steps of putting it on – slide it in, lower it, turn it right until it clicks shut). *Look at the lock* brings the camera close on it with the part set down beside it, so the neck and lugs and the socket's slots and tabs are both in view, a lug, a slot and a click tab each named on it; pressed again it goes to the next part's lock. *As printed*, beside *Spread* over the view (see the print check below), lays each piece on the bed as it prints and shows what the lock adds that overhangs past 45° in red and its bridges outlined. A cut across the whole model that asked to lock (*Locks*) where neither a clip nor the twist-lock fits has its *Locks* struck through and its row says *no lock fits here*: a removable part would hold.
- **A tea-light seat on the base.** A base taken out from underneath gets a round pocket on its top that a tea light stands in, centred, so it can't slide about while the lantern is carried: 39.5 mm across at the default clearance (the widest common tea light, 39 mm, with the clearance and a printer's wall error each side, so a 38 mm one sits within about 0.65 mm of the middle), 3 mm deep under the lowest point of the top round it, on a whole layer, with a 0.6 mm 45° lead-in round its mouth. It opens upwards, so the base still prints on its bottom with no support. It is asked for by default (the base's row has a *Tea-light seat Ø 39.5 mm* box, one undo step to switch, which stays there to untick whether a seat fits or not), and cut wherever it fits: a 38 mm tea light goes through the base's opening, the base keeps a wall of two lines round the seat, the top round it is level to within the seat's own depth (a generated model's ribbing is cut flat inside it), its floor stays a millimetre over the base's lock (2 mm with none), the base is solid under it, and the model leaves room over it for an LED tea light 45 mm tall with its flame. Where the model's own detail stands in the middle of the top prouder than the seat is deep – a candle cup on a lantern's floor, where the light is to go – the seat takes its place: the detail is cut away down to the seat's floor (and the base is listed as tall as it then is), so long as all of it lies inside the seat and it ends under the model over it. The card says *a seat for it on top, in place of the model's own detail there*, the row *tea-light seat Ø 39.5 mm in place of the model's own detail*, and the note how big it was and to untick *Tea-light seat* to keep it instead. Where no seat fits, the box's words are struck through and the card, the row and a note (to read, not a warning) say why – *it is too narrow to keep a wall round one – that takes a Ø 42.6 mm opening* – and nothing is cut. The pieces list, the notes and the glue sheet say *a 38 mm tea light sits in the seat on top of the base*, and how to fit a base a tea light goes into with the light standing on it, seat or none: hold the lantern up and twist the base in from underneath (turned over, the light would fall out). Use an LED tea light: a flame's heat softens the plastic over it. In the pieces view the seat is violet; a base's own ▶ shows it dropping out of the model's end, and *Look at the lock*, pressed again after the lock of a base a tea light goes into, becomes *Look at the seat* (or *Look at the base's top*, where it has none) and shows it from over the base – still there when the seat is switched or the base resized meanwhile. A part saved without the seat keeps none.
- **Suggest spots** lists the best places for a cut that comes apart, best first – along Z, or along the selected cut's own axis and with its hold when it comes apart (turn that cut, change its hold or select another, and the list is looked for again): *Z 190.5 mm – top 100.5 mm comes off · locks with 4 clips · under a ledge where the shape widens*. A good spot has a full lip with clips, in one piece (half a step over the tips of a row of windows, not just over them, where they would cut it into stretches), hides in a natural line the outside shows – by a ledge, a groove round a lid, or where straight sides start to slope (a roof flush with its walls, a jar's shoulder); the tips of a row of narrow windows are no such line, nor where one of several separate parts ends (a second box beside the first) – takes off a sensible part (a top, a lid or a cap – not a sliver, nor most of the model; along X or Y the smaller part, so a box lying on its side offers its lid as it does standing), runs through few separate parts (not fretwork, posts or a handle's legs) and through no letters. A spot is looked for on the side of a seam that leaves its shape whole – under a ledge the shape widens past, over one it narrows past – so a cut never leaves a thin plate of a band of gargoyles or an eaves on the other piece; a groove too narrow for a lip of its own is kept whole, the spot just under or over it. One that would run through letters is tried again just clear of them; where none is clear, the spot names the letters (*through “LIGHT”*). A spot whose flaws outweigh its lip is never listed. On a hold with clips where none fits anywhere (a round lantern's walls have no straight stretch for one, a small part none long enough), the spots are where the twist-lock takes their place, scored as clips are – and where it fits nowhere either, where a full 6 mm lip without them fits – and say so. The lip a spot promises is the one the download makes, on the piece the download puts it on – a slender tower's top is not stood on its small roof to carry it. The list also comes up when you tick *Comes apart* and when you start *+ Removable part*. Hover a spot to see its plane and section in the view (with the pieces shown, the model is drawn while you hover); click it to move the selected cut that comes apart there, or to add one there when none is selected – one undo step; a spot a cut already takes says so (*cut A is here*) and selects that cut instead, and a glued cut there comes apart. It is worked out only when asked (*Looking for good spots…* – about a second and a half on the square lantern refined to a million triangles, three to four seconds on a round fretwork lantern of a third of a million to a million, whose curved walls take longer to plan a lip on), a little at a time, so a cut being placed meanwhile gets its readout as it would anyway; a list worked out on the model alone, before the final result was built, is quietly worked out again on it, staying listed meanwhile. Where nothing fits it says *No good spot found* and offers *Place on model*, to pick one yourself.
- **Show pieces** works out the real pieces and pulls them apart along the cuts (*Spread* sets how far), with the pins in the gaps. Each is listed with its size, how it lies in its file, whether it fits and how many pins it has – or how many built-in pegs or sockets, and the lip of a cut that comes apart, or that it comes off at that cut; hover a row to pick out its piece. A piece that would have nothing flat to stand on (it rests on a curve or an edge) is pointed out: another cut through it gives it a face to lie on – or, when its only flat face is the one its built-in pegs stand out of (half a ball), pins let it lie on that face.
- **The print check.** Once the pieces are worked out, each has a line under it in the list that says what printing it the way its file lies will ask for – *Print check: may need supports: about 12 cm², standing on the bed · step lines about every 4 mm on a big face on top · its 4 pin holes need no supports* – and the glue sheet says the same beside each piece (it says once, in its heading, that the holes need no supports, and names a piece's pin holes only where some may need them). Nothing in the files changes for it; it is there so you know before you print. *Supports* go by the safe rule: any face flatter than 45° that is not on the bed may need them (a face drawn at exactly 45°, a chamfer, never counts). Your slicer often starts lower, at 30°, so it may ask for less – or, set another way, a little more. A level span with a wall under it at both ends is left to bridging: up to 10 mm it is not mentioned, and up to 30 mm, the span of the app's own clips, the line says *bridges a level span of about 24 mm* in place of counting it as supports (print with bridging on, the default; it may hang a little in the middle). Only a ceiling that really is level counts as a span: a roof that slopes, even by a couple of degrees, is laid in strips that hang from one side, so it is counted as needing supports. A ledge held along one side only is not a span either and is counted, however long it is, unless it stands no more than 2 mm out of its wall (the underside of a raised letter). A rounded bottom edge – a narrow band, 5 mm wide at most, that curves up off the bed and is steep enough within 3 mm – is left out too: nobody supports those. How finely a model is meshed makes no difference to any of this. The app's own holes, lips and locks are left out as well: they are shaped to print without. The line says whether the supports would stand on the bed or on the piece itself, where they mark the face below as well, and whether what they hold up is a glue face: that prints rough on supports and may want light sanding before you glue it (rare: a cut's face that looks down is normally the one the piece lies on). *Step lines*: a big face that leans a little off level (from a quarter of a degree to 15°) prints as a staircase of layers, with a line across it every few millimetres – about every 4 mm at 3° with 0.2 mm layers, every 2 mm with 0.1 mm layers. Another way under *Lay pieces*, or finer layers, shows them less. *May tip* and *small foot*: a piece more than five times as tall as its foot is wide, one whose weight hangs past its foot, or a tall one standing on very little – add a brim in your slicer, 5 mm wide, or 8 mm for a very tall piece. A thin tube standing on its ring is not told so: it stands on little, but all round. A round bar lying down *rests on a narrow strip* and wants a brim too. Only what is worth a note gets one, one note a piece at most: supports under 5 cm² or more of the model's own faces (less stays on the piece's line) or under 1 cm² of a glue face, a big face with step lines, a piece that may tip or come loose (*Piece 3 has a big face on top (about 80 cm²) that leans 3° off level: at 0.2 mm layers it prints with a step line about every 4 mm. Another way to lay it, or finer layers, shows them less.*). A piece laid *Best side down* is not told to lay it another way for its supports, as that is the way that needs least. A model downloaded whole is checked the same way, standing as it is modelled, and the download message says what was found.
- **As printed.** Whenever the pieces are shown, any split, tick *As printed* beside *Spread* over the view: each piece is laid on the grid the way its file lies on the bed, in a row. The print check is painted on them, from the very faces it counted, so the picture shows red, amber or step lines only on a piece whose line speaks of them: faces that *may need supports* are red (they look down, so turn the view to see them from below), supports under a glue face a paler red, a *level span to bridge* amber, and where a piece's line tells of *step lines* they are drawn as dark lines across every face on top that leans a little – all the way round a piece of a shallow cone or dome, not only on its biggest patch – about where the printer will leave them: where the face crosses the middle of each layer. A leaning underside gets none; it prints on supports. (A dome or cone whose line does not speak of step lines gets none drawn either, though it will show them – see the limitations.) Lines that would sit closer than about three dots of your screen are thinned – every second one drawn, or every fourth – and the legend then reads *step lines, about here (not all drawn)*. A legend over the view names each colour, and only the ones painted on the pieces (some may be on an underside you have to turn the view to see). A piece whose line says *no supports needed* has nothing red on it. On a model with a twist-lock the lock's own overhang and bridges are shown as before. Untick it, or leave the pieces view, and the pieces are spread apart again. It only shows: nothing in the files changes.
- **Choose how a piece lies.** Once the pieces are worked out, each has a small *Lies on* choice under it in the list. It starts at *The app's choice* and says which side that is (and goes on saying it after you pick another, so you can compare – except with built-in pegs, where the app's own choice depends on which piece carries them). Pick another – *As in the model*, *Upside down*, *On its left side*, *On its right side*, *On its front*, *On its back* (left, right, front and back as you see the model when the view starts, or after *Reset view*), with *(a cut face)* after a side that is one – and the piece is worked out again lying on that side: its pin holes are drilled again for the new way up (round where they now stand upright, a teardrop with its point up where they now lie), its size and whether it fits follow, and so do its *Print check* and *Estimate* lines, *As printed*, and its PRINT line on the sheet, which adds *as you chose*. Each side says about how much of the piece would rest on the bed and how much would need support – the figures the app goes by when it chooses for itself; the *Print check* line, made from the finished piece, has the last word. A side the piece cannot take is greyed out and says why: it doesn't fit your printer that way, part of it would hang in the air, it has to lie flat (a thin plate, or a piece beside a dovetail joint), a part comes off it so it prints standing as modelled, its lip has to print standing up, or its take-apart joint is made for another side. Built-in pegs rule out no side: where they are in the way – they stand out of the side you pick, or the piece would be too big for your printer with them – the entry ends *its pegs go to the piece it joins*, and they do. Where that piece is itself held to a side that leaves the pegs no room (you chose its side too, or its lip sets it), the entry says so instead – *its joint with piece 2 would have no pegs – glued flat* – and if you pick it all the same, the warning says which pieces' sides are why and that picking *The app's choice* for one of them brings the pegs back. A part that comes off, and a part of the model that prints as more than one piece (a bookcase's loose shelves between two cuts), have no choice; if you chose a side before it came to print that way, a note says it is not used, and its first piece keeps the list so you can pick *The app's choice* again. A piece lying on a side you chose is marked *set by you* (point at the closed list to read its whole entry), and the notes name it (*Piece 2 lies on the side you chose in the pieces list: its pin holes and print check are made for that side, so print it as the file lies.*). Choose the side here rather than turning a piece in your slicer: the holes are right only for the side the file lies on. The choices are kept while the cuts make the same pieces – drag a cut as far as you like – and dropped when a cut is added, removed, turned to another axis or left on the other side of another cut (dragged past it and back in one go, they stay), when *Fit to my printer* makes new cuts (pressed again with nothing changed it makes the same cuts, and they stay), or the model is turned, moved or resized, because the pieces are then no longer the same ones; a note says so (*The cuts changed, so the pieces are no longer the same ones: the side you chose for a piece to lie on was dropped…*), and Undo brings them back. With nothing chosen, every file is exactly what it was.
- **The print plan.** Once the pieces are worked out, each also has an *Estimate* line – *about 33–42 g · about 11–14 m of filament · very roughly 1–5 hours* – and under the list the whole job: its weight, how much filament that is and what share of a 1 kg spool, a very rough time for all of it, the tallest piece, the largest one on the bed, and how much of your bed the pieces would cover side by side. The piece files are solid models; your slicer is what prints them mostly hollow, with a few walls outside and a light pattern (the infill) inside – so the weight is worked out for the *Material* (PLA, PETG, ABS / ASA or TPU) and the *Infill* (15 % to start with) you choose under *Printing*, with 2 walls, your nozzle and your layer height. Changing either changes the figures at once and nothing else: no file is touched. Weight and filament are given as a range (from a tenth under to 15 % over what the sum comes to, and never more than the piece would weigh printed solid); the time is said only *very roughly*, in whole hours and as a wide band – in whole days of printing for a very long job – for a fast printer when one of the Bambu Lab or Prusa printers is chosen and an ordinary one otherwise. The time follows your layer height and nozzle: layers half as high take about twice as long, and a wide nozzle with thick layers is quicker. Supports, a brim and waste from colour changes are not counted. *Suggestions for your slicer*, folded away under the job, are a handful of plain ones: the walls and infill that are plenty for a model on a shelf (and 3 walls, not more infill, for one that is handled), which pieces are mostly walls, so that the infill changes little, that 3 or 4 walls make built-in pegs stronger (and that this adds to that piece's estimate), which pieces want a brim and which may need supports – whether supports *on the build plate only* are enough – both taken from each piece's own print check so the two never disagree, that pins print solid, and a word on PETG (only where your Clearance is under 0.20 mm), ABS / ASA and TPU where it matters. The sheet in the zip has the same as a **PRINT PLAN** block after its PRINT lines, for the material and infill chosen when you download – all but the word on the pins, which the sheet's own *pins* line just above it already has (*lying on a flat side, solid (4+ walls)*). A model printed whole gets its estimate in the Model card once it has been shown or downloaded, and on the download's status line.
- **Pins.** Every face where a cut meets the model gets blind holes in both pieces – at least two where there is room, in a pattern that fits only one way round – kept a wall's width from the sides, from engraving and pockets, and from the holes of every other cut. The pins are octagons printed lying on a flat: strong, no supports, and the flats leave room for glue and air. The holes have a chamfered mouth for squeezed-out glue and a pointed end, and are teardrop-shaped where the piece prints them lying down, so no hole needs supports either. Where a hole's point would come too close to an edge or to another hole, that hole's point is cut off flat just above it – a short level roof (up to about 3.5 mm wide on the largest pins, less on the smaller sizes), one layer over the hole and 0.2 mm at least, which every printer bridges without supports – so the face keeps its pins and the full wall round them; the pin fits the same, and a note says how many pins on which cut got such holes (nothing is said of filament holes, whose flat is too small to see). A hole whose point has the room keeps it, even on a thin face: one near the bottom of a thin plate, or in a wall that is thin from side to side. *Auto* picks one pin size for the whole model, the largest of 8, 6, 5, 4 and 3 mm that fits: no more than a third of the width of the narrowest face it has to go in (a flat-sided face 15 mm thick takes 5 mm pins, one of 14.9 mm takes 4 mm; a round bar has to be about twice as thick for the same pins), with room for two full-length pins there – counting a flat-topped hole where a pointed one has no room. The width is the face's own, also where the side curves or leans away behind the cut (a tube cut along its length; a face that closes a hollow counts only as wide as the wall round it): each size is tried with its own hole, and a smaller pin's shorter hole stays inside where a bigger one's would come out of the side. Where two cuts are so close together that the holes of one leave the other no room for that size, *Auto* goes down a size or two until both cuts get their pins, rather than leave one of them with filament. Bigger pins stand further apart, so a face can get one pin fewer than it would with a smaller size; a face too narrow for it takes shorter pins, then 1.75 mm filament, then glue alone, and the notes say which. *My own dowels* drills the same holes and leaves out the pin file, for wooden or steel dowels.
- **Pins that stay on the bed.** A pin lying on its narrow flat touches the bed along a thin line, and a sheet of them can come loose halfway through the print. So the pins file has a thin strip under every column of pins – 0.2 mm thick, one first layer – that reaches 5 mm past the pins on every side and joins the column into one part with plenty of bed under it. The pins themselves are exactly the same, and in the same places. Print the file as it loads, with the first layer set to 0.2 mm and no brim. Moving the whole file about the bed is fine; just don't split it into separate parts in the slicer, or the strips can end up pushed against each other. Then peel each strip off the bed and snap the pins out of it. The strip tears rather than breaking off clean, so trim off any edge of it left on a pin's flat – a fingernail or a knife does it – or the pin will be tight in its hole. The strips of two columns stay 2 mm apart. Because a sheet is 10 mm bigger each way with its strips, a full sheet holds a row or a column of pins fewer on some beds, and the rest go on another sheet. The fit test's pin lies on the same strip. The notes, the sheet in the zip and the print plan's weight all take the strip into account. *Hold printed pins down with a thin strip*, under *Printing*, is on to start with and remembered; turn it off and the pins file and the fit test are exactly as they were without it.
- **Built-in pegs.** Joints: *Built-in pegs* prints the pegs as part of the pieces: one piece of each pair carries all of a face's pegs, standing out of it, and the other has matching sockets – no pins to print, no dowels to buy, nothing to lose. The pegs go where pins would – a wall's width from the sides, from pockets and from the pegs and sockets of other cuts – with matching labels. Only the socket needs room inside its piece; the peg needs only a wall's thickness of solid under its root (2–4 mm behind the face, by size), so when one piece is too thin for sockets and the other has the room, the thin one carries the pegs: a plaque cut through its 5 mm base gets pegs standing up on the base, which lies on its underside, and sockets where the top is thick enough (under the rim and the raised logo, not under engraved letters). It does so only where it then prints about as well as it would glued – on at least half as much of the bed, needing support under no more than a tenth of it more: a lid with a knob, which could then only stand on its edge or rest on the knob, is glued instead, and a thin plate between two cuts carries the pegs of one face and lies on the other, which is glued. Each is a square turned 45° on the face, so a piece that prints it lying down has a diamond, with 45° sides under it and over it, and neither peg nor socket needs supports whichever way its piece lies; the peg is exactly its size with a chamfered tip, the socket has the clearance, a chamfered mouth, room for glue past the peg and a pointed end. The piece that carries a face's pegs never lies on that face, and is never the one whose neighbour has no room for the sockets: otherwise, if one piece of the pair lies on it, the other carries them; if both would, the one that prints about as well lying another way; if neither does, the one that prints them lying down. Where both pieces have room for the sockets but one has more (more of the faces, larger pegs or longer ones), the pegs go across from it whenever the piece that then carries them prints about as well: a box's 8 mm lid lies on its top carrying them, so the sockets go deep into the walls, and a bar with a hollow behind the face on one side gets its sockets in the solid half – but never a piece the pegs would make too big for your printer while the other has room for them, and a piece made of loose parts (two prongs cut off a fork) only where every part of it still rests on the bed. *Auto* picks one size for the whole model, the largest of 8, 6, 5 and 4 mm that fits the piece each face's sockets go into (a face that ends up glued has no say), and the pegs stand out 1.5 × their size (a 6 mm peg 9 mm); a face too narrow for them takes smaller pegs, then shorter ones, then glue alone – never pins – and the notes say which. The pegs make their piece longer: its size in the list and the fit on the bed count them, and so do the boxes between the cuts, the red tint, the drag readout and *Fit to my printer* – on the side of each cut that will carry them (the piece below it, or the one above where only that one has room), as long as the longest peg a face that size takes. *Fit to my printer* moves a cut a little towards the piece that carries its pegs where that lets both fit, and plans the pieces lying as they would with pins where it can, so a slab's quarters still lie flat. A cut through a thin plate still gets dovetail tabs. Printed pins are still the strongest joint: a peg that prints pointing up (on a piece standing on its end, such as a tube or a U bracket's leg, or on a thin base that carries them on its top) is weaker across its layers than a pin, which always prints lying down; the notes say when a cut's pegs all print standing up, and name pins as stronger only where their holes would fit.
- **Dovetails through thin plates.** A cut through a plate 2–10 mm thick (a sign, a plaque) has no room for pins, so *Auto* gives it dovetail tabs through the thickness: both pieces print flat and go together by lowering one onto the other. Tabs are only used where both pieces can lie flat on the plate: through the legs of a U bracket one leg would be held up over the bed, so that cut gets pins. They go only where the plate is solid right through, so a sealed pocket or an engraving beside the cut stays whole in one piece. A plate thinner than 2 mm is a flat glue joint.
- **Matching labels.** The same label – the piece's number and the cut's letter, *1A* on one face and *2A* on the other – is engraved 0.6 mm deep in the same spot on both faces of every joint with pins or built-in pegs, clear of the holes, pegs and sockets, reading upright from outside: put each label on its match. Dovetail and glue-only joints get no labels, nor do cuts that come apart.
- **The download** is one zip: a file for each piece, turned the way it prints best and standing on the bed, every part of it – the loose parts of a box that would be held over the bed lying with the rest (the shelves of a bookcase between two cuts) are pieces of their own, with their own labels and glue steps, and counted as such by the offer and the download button; each one's pins go where it has material, and one hooked round the rest of its box (a ribbon's end round a column) goes on last: pushed onto its pins from beyond the cut once the rest is on, or, where it cannot go on along them that way either, glued with none, from the way it can go on (from the front, say), the notes saying where to move the cut to keep it on; a bit a cut would leave loose that hangs on the piece across the cut – a crumb of a letter, the tip of a tail, a fin of no more than a quarter of its box's largest part – stays on that piece instead, in one part with it, where it can come away from its own box along the cut and the piece still fits your printer, and the notes say so (one that would make the piece too big goes onto it first, before the rest of its box, and the glue sheet says so); a crumb that touches only such bits goes with them; the pins, with a spare for every four pins or part of four (`…-pins-3x-6x19mm.stl`, on more than one sheet when your bed can't take them all, each column of pins on a thin strip that holds it to the bed unless you turned that off; none with built-in pegs, which are part of their pieces); any part connected with pegs; and `…-how-to-glue.txt` with the print list – each piece with what the print check found of it, and a reminder to print each piece lying the way its file lies and not to tip it onto another side in your slicer (spinning it flat on the bed is fine) – a dry-fit check and the glue order, cut by cut – the cuts that come apart last, not glued. Where the split takes out a part that comes off, the files are named after the model (`batlantern-2-pieces.zip`, `batlantern-piece-1-of-2.stl`), with `-text` where there is text on it, and where it glues nothing the sheet is `…-how-to-print-and-fit.txt`, as that is all it says to do; a split with no such part keeps the names its downloads always had (`…-text-…`, `…-how-to-glue.txt`). *Download fit test* says in its tooltip what this split's fit test holds – a ring and a plug with the base's twist-lock, say – and what to check once it is printed shows whole under the buttons. Text crossing a cut is split with the model (a note names the pieces either side wherever the cut splits letters); a sealed hollow inside a piece (a pocket for a magnet, a void that saves material) stays hollow; triangles that couldn't be repaired are cut with their piece but get no pins or labels. Raised text on its own, with no model, can be split as well.

Print it first: the pin should slide into both holes, the peg push into its socket and the tab drop into its socket by hand, and the lid click on and pull off by hand – or, where the cut locks, lock on and come off only with a firm pull; the rings should twist together with a click and hold when pulled, and the plug, its coin slot towards you, go into the ring's bottom turned to the left and turn right until it clicks. Too tight (a lock too stiff to open), raise the clearance by 0.05 mm; too loose, or a lid with no click, lower it.

### Tips for printing

- Choose your **material** and **infill** under *Printing* to see about how much plastic and time a model or a split takes; they change the estimate only, never a file.
- Set your **nozzle** and **layer height** under *Printing*; the warnings use them. With a 0.4 mm nozzle keep strokes ≥ 0.8 mm (use **Boldness** to thicken thin fonts) and raised text ≥ 0.4 mm tall, cut-in text ≥ 0.4 mm deep.
- **Round sharp corners** rounds every corner by half the nozzle width, which prints more cleanly; the radius is limited automatically so thin letters are never erased.
- Text wraps up to about 120° either side of where you click (two thirds of the way round a cane). Longer text would run round the back – the app warns; use shorter text, smaller letters or two lines.
- **Overlap** (under *Advanced*) is how far the text sinks into or pokes out of the surface so the merge is clean. The default 0.4 mm is fine almost everywhere.

### Limitations

- WOFF2 and `.ttc` font collections can't be read – use the `.ttf` / `.otf` / `.woff` version of the font.
- Cutting text into a model needs a watertight solid. Repair fixes most files; parts that stay open are kept in your download but can only have text raised on them.
- The built-in fonts contain Latin characters only. For other scripts, upload a font that has them.
- Text placed on another text item is positioned relative to the model surface, not the other text. On a surface curved two ways (a sphere) a long line of text keeps its letters' size along its middle, and letters far above or below it are stretched or squeezed a little – the app warns when that becomes noticeable.
- Splitting cuts only straight across X, Y or Z, never at an angle. It needs a solid model: a model that couldn't be repaired at all can't be split, and on one with some triangles left unrepaired those triangles get no pins or labels and no cut gets dovetails. Dovetails only go through thin plates; everywhere else *Dovetails* uses pins. Built-in pegs are weaker than printed pins, above all where a piece prints them pointing up (they break across their layers): use pins for a joint that takes a load. How firmly a lip clicks or locks is worked out, not measured: print the fit test. A lip goes only on a piece that can print with it standing up, and two cuts that come apart should not cross: each lip is planned as if the other were not there. *Largest piece* is for the pieces *Fit to my printer* plans: a bit of the model that a cut gives back to the piece across it, so it does not print loose, can make that piece a little longer (the notes say so), and the corner an X1 or P1 keeps clear is kept only by the *X1 / P1S / P1P* preset – a bed size of your own (*Custom*) has none.
- Pin holes are shaped for the way each piece lies in its file: print a piece turned another way and a hole made round to stand up may lie on its side and need supports. A piece that had to keep a way to lie for its pin holes says so in the notes (*Piece 2 lies on a cut face: its pin holes were made to print that way, so don't turn it in your slicer.*). *Auto* never picks a pin wider than a third of the face, even where a bigger one would keep its wall – pick the *Pin size* yourself to get the larger one (a bar 13 mm thick takes 4 mm pins with *Auto*, 5 mm ones when you ask for them). *Auto* wants pins of full length: on a face with air close behind it, a bigger size picked by hand may still fit as shorter pins (the notes say so) where *Auto* takes the smaller size at full length. Where two cuts are close together, *Auto* goes down a size only to save a face from filament or glue alone; pins that had to be made shorter to pass the other cut's holes stay as they are, with a note. On a face that only just has room for two pins, the layer height can decide between one pin size and the next, because a flat-topped hole is one layer taller than the pin: change the layer height and *Auto* may show another size. The fit test's lying hole is always the pointed one; a flat-topped hole takes the pin the same.
- *Lies on* offers the six straight sides only: it cannot tip a piece a few degrees to level a leaning face. Laying a piece another way can move the pins on its faces a little, make a hole flat-topped, or change the size where *Pin size* is *Auto* – a hole lying on its side needs more room to an edge than one standing up – so the pieces it joins are drilled again as well: download all the pieces again, not only the one you turned. With built-in pegs, a piece told to lie on the face its pegs would stand out of, or on a side where it would be too big for your printer with them, hands them to the piece across the cut – which may then lie another way itself, so its row can change though you did not touch it – and where neither piece can carry them, that face is glued flat (the notes say so). A piece you lay on a curved side gets a warning that it rests on a curve or an edge and needs supports; another side cures it. A lip or a twist-lock goes on the piece it prints best on whatever side was chosen: that piece lies as its lip needs, its row reads *set by you, but not used*, and a note says why. A side chosen earlier that the piece can no longer take – a smaller printer, a cut moved so that it no longer fits – is not used either, with a note; and while a cut misses the model or lies where another one is, no chosen side is used. The figures beside each side are counted on the piece before its holes are drilled, roughly, and are for comparing sides. Choices are not kept from one visit to the next, like the rest of the split.
- The strip that holds printed pins down is 0.2 mm thick whatever layer height you chose, because that is the usual first layer: with a first layer up to about 0.3 mm it still prints as one layer, and with a thinner one as two. A first layer thicker than that (a wide nozzle often comes with one of 0.4 mm) and the slicer may leave the strip out altogether, without a word, so set the first layer to 0.2 mm for this file. It is meant to be printed as the file loads – add a brim and the strips grow together; split the file into separate parts and the slicer may push them against each other. The strip has no line to break along, so it tears and can leave a thin edge on the pin's flat: trim that off before you try the pin in its hole, and before you blame the clearance for a tight pin. On a bed only just long enough for a pin the strip no longer fits, and the notes say so: turn the strip off under *Printing*, or use dowels. On a bed narrower than a pin and its strip are long, the pins file is laid out to be turned a quarter on the bed.
- The print check is an estimate, not a slicer. It goes by 45° whatever your slicer is set to, counts area as seen from below, and judges where supports would stand from the middle of each face. It looks at the solid pieces only (not at triangles left unrepaired), takes a piece's weight as if it were printed solid, and calls a level span *bridged* by measuring from the wall under it on one side to the wall on the other, not by trying it: a 30 mm bridge prints on most printers but not on all. A ceiling counts as level within 1° and a layer's height (0.3 mm) over all of it, so the bumpy ceiling of a scanned model is counted as needing supports, to be safe. Where supports would stand – on the bed or on the piece – is told to about a five-hundredth of the piece's width. It only reports: it never changes how a piece lies. A model downloaded whole with more than two million triangles is not checked, and a piece the check cannot be made for simply has no line.
- The print plan's figures are estimates; your slicer's are the real ones. The weight takes a skin two lines thick all round (top and bottom the same as the walls, where a slicer makes them a little thicker) and your infill inside it, so a piece with many thin walls or fine detail may come out heavier than said, and one your slicer prints with fewer top layers lighter. The time is a rule of thumb – a line as wide as your nozzle and as high as your layer, laid at 30–60 mm a second on an ordinary printer and 75–165 on a fast one, never more plastic than a hot end melts, and a few seconds a layer besides – and has not been measured on any printer: take it as "an evening" or "a weekend", not as a time to plan by. Walls are fixed at 2 for the estimate; there is no setting for them. Supports, a brim and colour changes are left out, and so are triangles that could not be repaired. Material and infill are only for the estimate: the app does not change Clearance for a material, it only suggests it.
- *As printed* draws the step lines *about* where they will fall: it takes the first layer to be as thick as the others, and your slicer may start the layers a little higher or lower. It draws them only on a piece whose line tells of step lines, and there on every shallow face on top, the small ones too; a rounded top with no single big leaning face – a dome, or a whole shallow cone, printed in one piece – has no step lines told or drawn, though it will show them plainly: a plain top in this view does not promise a smooth one. The same top cut in two can have them told and drawn on each half, because half of it may count as one big face. How many lines are thinned out goes by the size of the window when you tick the box, not by how far you have zoomed in since. A very dense split is shown through lighter copies of the pieces, and is painted from the same check run on those copies, so a red edge can sit a hair off the real one; whether a piece has red on it at all still goes by its line. Pieces stay solid in this view (only a twist-lock's pieces fade round the lock), so faces that need supports are seen from below. Unrepaired triangles, pins and separately printed parts are not painted.
- Very large models (millions of triangles) need a lot of memory. Use **Simplify** in *Fix model* first – the app suggests it above a million triangles.

## Publishing on GitHub Pages

The workflow in [`.github/workflows/deploy.yml`](.github/workflows/deploy.yml) runs the tests, builds the app with Vite and deploys `dist/` to GitHub Pages on every push to `main` or the development branch (the branch names are listed in the `if:` conditions of that file).

1. In the repository go to **Settings → Pages** and set **Source** to **GitHub Actions**.
2. Push (or re-run the workflow from the Actions tab). The app appears at `https://<your-user>.github.io/<repo>/`.

Don't use the "Jekyll" starter workflow GitHub suggests: this app has to be *built* (the browser cannot load `src/` and `node_modules/` directly), so a Jekyll deployment would publish a blank page.

The page's own address is not versioned the way its scripts are, so a browser may serve it from its cache for a while after a deploy, and a tab left open runs the version it was opened with. Each build writes `version.json` beside the page (the commit it was built from); the page asks for it, never from a cache, when it starts and whenever it is shown again, and where the site has a newer build it says so once, with **Reload**.

| `src/style.css`, `src/cyber.css`, `src/theme.js` | The page's two looks: `style.css` is the plain Classic one, `cyber.css` the cyberpunk one laid over it (every rule scoped to `html:not([data-theme='classic'])`), and `theme.js` the switch – what the browser remembers (`stltext.theme`), and the icon and browser colour of each look. The 3D view's text colours follow it (`Viewer.setTheme`, `TEXT_COLORS`) |

Built with [three.js](https://threejs.org), [three-mesh-bvh](https://github.com/gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh), [opentype.js](https://opentype.js.org), [Manifold](https://github.com/elalish/manifold) and [Vite](https://vite.dev). Needs a browser with module Web Workers and WebGL (current Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari).
